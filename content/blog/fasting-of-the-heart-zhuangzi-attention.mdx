# The Fasting of the Heart: Zhuangzi's Attention Practice for an Overloaded Age

### What *xinzhai* and “sitting and forgetting” reveal about receptivity, interpretation, and the danger of turning an ancient text into a modern technique

The [Universal Transmissions companion essay](https://www.universal-transmissions.com/journal/fasting-of-the-heart-zhuangzi-attention) takes the fasting of the heart-mind into the studio: how to protect a pause before judgment, how to listen before solving, and how an ancient image can become a modest contemporary practice without pretending to be medicine. This Vault of Arcana edition stays with the texts and their history. It asks what the *Zhuangzi* actually gives us when it describes **心齋** (*xinzhai*, usually translated “fasting of the mind” or “fasting of the heart-mind”) and **坐忘** (*zuowang*, “sitting and forgetting”).

The short answer is not a meditation brand. It is an argument about how a human being becomes capable of meeting a world that does not already agree with them.

That distinction is necessary because *Zhuangzi* is routinely recruited into modern projects it never explicitly names. It is presented as a manual for mindfulness, a proof of quantum consciousness, an early psychology, a prescription for productivity, or a Chinese synonym for “go with the flow.” Each comparison may contain a suggestive echo. None should be mistaken for the text's own historical identity.

The *Zhuangzi* is a composite work associated with the philosopher Zhuang Zhou and with layers of writing and commentary that accumulated around the Inner Chapters and later material. Its stories argue through paradox, humor, transformation, dream, debate, reversal, and deliberate destabilization. It does not hand us one systematic doctrine with a clean technical vocabulary. The text's resistance to final capture is part of its method.

So the thesis of this essay must remain carefully bounded: in chapters 4 and 6, the *Zhuangzi* uses the images of fasting and forgetting to explore a form of self-cultivation in which the heart-mind loosens its premature grip on distinctions, roles, and interpretations. That loosening is not blankness, passivity, or loss of discernment. It is the creation of a receptive interval in which response can be adjusted to the situation rather than dictated by the self's first position.

## The scene: a dangerous ruler and a dangerous certainty

The first relevant passage occurs in “In the World Among Human Beings,” the fourth chapter of the *Zhuangzi*. Yan Hui wishes to visit the ruler of Wei, whose conduct is presented as violent and self-satisfied. Yan Hui proposes ways to advise him. Confucius rejects them one by one. The point is not that ethical speech is useless. It is that a speaker who enters a distorted relation while carrying the confidence of the correct answer may only add another force to the situation.

The dialogue is not an abstract meditation placed outside history. It is about how to move among power, danger, persuasion, and self-deception. Yan Hui thinks he can go with a program. Confucius asks what kind of person would be going.

The answer is fasting. Confucius tells Yan Hui to fast the heart-mind before he proceeds. The Chinese phrase **心齋** is compact but difficult. *Xin* does not map neatly onto the modern division between “heart” as emotion and “mind” as reason. In early Chinese philosophical contexts it can name the center of perception, feeling, intention, and thought — the organ, literal or figurative, by which a person is oriented. *Zhai* suggests fasting, abstaining, or ritual purification. “Fasting of the mind” is serviceable English, but it can make the practice sound like an attack on cognition. “Fasting of the heart-mind” retains the ambiguity and the difficulty.

The context tells us what kind of difficulty is at stake. Yan Hui does not need more arguments. He needs to stop carrying himself into the encounter as a closed answer.

The passage is preserved in the [Chinese Text Project's chapter text](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/human-world), where translations can be compared with the original. Readers should notice the range of translation rather than treating one English sentence as transparent access to the classical Chinese. This is not pedantry. In a text that makes perspective and naming central problems, translation is part of the interpretation.

## Three ways of listening

Confucius describes a progression. First there is listening with the ears: receiving sound as sound. Then there is listening with the mind: receiving meaning, concepts, and interpretations. But the instruction moves beyond both toward listening with **qi**.

This last term is notoriously difficult to translate. It can refer to breath, vital energy, atmosphere, disposition, or an animating process, depending on context and tradition. In the *Zhuangzi* passage it should not be turned into a warrant for every modern claim made in the name of “energy.” The textual point is more exacting: a person can be present to a situation through more than the isolated sensory input and more than the conceptual position built on that input.

Listening with the ears says: what was said?

Listening with the mind says: what does it mean, and how does it fit my categories?

Listening with qi suggests: what is the whole situation doing, and can I remain responsive without forcing it into the first interpretation available to me?

The sequence is not a simple hierarchy. The ears are not inferior, and the mind is not an obstacle to be destroyed. Without sensory attention, the practice becomes fantasy. Without thought, it cannot distinguish a real correction from a passing mood. The movement is instead from reception, through interpretation, toward a more distributed responsiveness in which body, feeling, context, and consequence remain in the field.

The text then turns to emptiness. The heart-mind becomes like an empty room. This image is often treated as a promise of interior vacancy. But a room is empty in order to receive. Its emptiness is functional, not nihilistic. A chamber with no available space cannot host a guest, a conversation, or a change of arrangement.

That image is a useful guardrail. *Xinzhai* does not mean becoming a person with no preferences or judgments. It means that preferences and judgments are not allowed to occupy every square inch of the encounter before the encounter has happened.

## Fasting is not suppression

Modern readers often hear “fasting the mind” as “stop thinking.” That interpretation creates a familiar spiritual contest: the successful practitioner defeats mental noise and achieves a superior quiet. The *Zhuangzi* gives us no reliable reason to make that the goal.

A fasting person still needs to perceive danger, understand speech, remember obligations, and decide how to act. Yan Hui is not preparing to become inert. He is preparing to enter a politically charged encounter without allowing the desire to be right to govern every movement.

The practice is therefore closer to relinquishing ownership than to erasing content. A thought may appear. An emotion may rise. A judgment may be justified. The question is whether the person must immediately make the thought, emotion, or judgment the sovereign of the situation.

This distinction matters because “emptying” can be used in two opposite ways. It can loosen an ego's demand for control, or it can become another technology of control — a performance in which the practitioner polices every inner event and condemns the self for failing to be still. The second version looks disciplined but remains organized around possession. It wants to own emptiness.

The *Zhuangzi* repeatedly makes this kind of capture unstable. Its famous stories do not simply tell the reader what to believe; they shift the position from which the reader believes. The butterfly dream, the crooked tree, the skillful butcher, the debates about knowing the happiness of fish: each story makes a settled point of view feel less secure without replacing it with a final point of view. The text's philosophy is performed through destabilization.

That is why the best contemporary gloss on *xinzhai* must remain modest: let the first interpretation become provisional enough for the situation to answer back.

## “Sitting and forgetting” in chapter 6

A second passage appears in “The Great and Most Honoured Master,” chapter 6. Yan Hui tells Confucius that he has made progress in practice. At first he has forgotten benevolence and righteousness. Then he has forgotten rites and music. Finally he says he is sitting and forgetting.

The [Chinese Text Project version of chapter 6](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/great-and-most-honoured-master) lets us see the exchange in context. Confucius asks what “sitting and forgetting” means. Yan Hui answers with a language of dropping away bodily form, dismissing intelligence, and leaving distinctions behind while becoming joined to the great pervader. Confucius approves the progress and asks to be allowed to follow.

The passage is powerful and dangerous to summarize. “Forgetting” can sound like an instruction to abandon moral discrimination, social responsibility, or embodied life. Yet the story does not simply praise carelessness. It stages a loosening of the identities and distinctions through which a person tries to secure a fixed position. Yan Hui is not boasting that he has acquired a new doctrine. He is reporting that certain supports no longer have the same grip.

The relationship between *xinzhai* and *zuowang* is interpretive rather than mechanical. The text does not present a standardized two-step course called “fasting, then sitting and forgetting.” Both passages nevertheless explore a related question: what happens when the self's ordinary way of organizing reality is interrupted?

In chapter 4, the interruption makes receptivity possible before a dangerous encounter. In chapter 6, the interruption reaches the roles and distinctions by which a person recognizes and defends the self. One image concerns the chamber that must be cleared enough to receive. The other concerns the structures of identity that no longer need to be clenched.

Neither should be reduced to dissociation. The test of a reading is what kind of person it produces in the world. If “forgetting” means becoming less able to recognize harm, keep promises, or respect another's boundaries, it has not become wisdom. If “emptiness” means surrendering judgment to whoever claims spiritual authority, it has become a technique of capture.

## Zhuangzi is not one timeless meditation system

The broad category “Daoism” contains philosophical texts, religious movements, ritual systems, lineages of bodily cultivation, alchemical traditions, commentarial practices, and modern reconstructions. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's overview of Daoism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/daoism/) is useful precisely because it makes this diversity difficult to ignore.

The *Zhuangzi* is an important source for later Daoist reflection, but later Daoist traditions are not simply the *Zhuangzi* continued without change. The vocabulary of qi, emptiness, fasting, inner training, breath cultivation, meditation, and inner alchemy travels across periods and genres. It acquires new technical meanings in new settings.

Harold Roth's *Original Tao* is valuable here because it places the early *Neiye* (“Inward Training”) material in relation to the foundations of Daoist mystical practice rather than treating every early text as one undifferentiated manual. Brook Ziporyn's translation of the *Zhuangzi* likewise preserves the work's strangeness and its commentarial afterlife without pretending that every paradox resolves into a modern therapeutic proposition.

The [Stanford Encyclopedia entry on Zhuangzi](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2002/entries/zhuangzi/) offers another necessary caution. The work is not easy to convert into a single doctrinal system because its arguments are made through perspectival shifts, irony, and literary indirection. “What does Zhuangzi believe?” is sometimes less productive than “What does this story make the reader unable to keep believing in the same way?”

That question changes how the fasting passage should be used. It is not a credential for importing a favored system of breathwork, neuroscience, productivity, or energy medicine into an ancient text. It is a textual event that asks what happens when a person stops treating their own first perspective as the measure of the encounter.

## The relation to inner cultivation

Readers interested in inner alchemy will notice a family resemblance. The broader history of Daoist cultivation often treats the human being as a site in which attention, breath, vitality, spirit, and ethical orientation can be refined. The *Zhuangzi* gives us a vocabulary for loosening the rigid self that obstructs transformation. Later traditions develop more elaborate maps, techniques, bodily theories, ritual settings, and lineages.

Family resemblance is not identity.

The [Microcosmic Orbit essay on Universal Transmissions](https://www.universal-transmissions.com/journal/taoist-microcosmic-orbit-inner-alchemy) examines a later and more explicitly circulatory image: attention and vitality trained through a return around the body's central pathways. The present essay begins earlier and elsewhere. It asks how a heart-mind becomes receptive before any circulation can be trusted. The orbit is about patterned movement; *xinzhai* is about the reduction of premature interference.

For readers who want to compare Taoist classics, inner-alchemy manuals, dream practice, Qigong, Taiji, and related materials, the [Taoist Inner Alchemy & Dream Practice Archive](https://www.vaultofarcana.com/shop/etsy-4543082389) is a relevant research path. It is a digital study archive, not a single-authority canon. Its usefulness lies in comparison: seeing which claims belong to which period, practice, or genre rather than allowing the word “Taoist” to flatten them all into one promise.

That distinction is especially important for bodily practices. Nothing in the *Zhuangzi* passages cited here establishes a medical protocol, guarantees a physiological effect, or authorizes breath retention and strenuous exercise. A reflective adaptation should remain voluntary, ordinary, and reversible. Historical study and health advice are different forms of work.

## A reading practice rather than a prescription

If *xinzhai* is to be brought into the present, the safest translation is not “do this ritual and achieve this state.” It is a reading practice with three movements.

**First, separate contact from conclusion.** Read the passage in a reliable translation and, where possible, compare the Chinese or multiple translations. Write down what the text actually says before writing what you think it means. This is the scholarly version of listening with the ears.

**Second, identify the self's investment.** What do you need the passage to prove? That ancient China discovered mindfulness? That your preferred energy system is universal? That you are already receptive? The answer may reveal more about the modern reader than about Zhuangzi. This is the fasting gesture: loosening the claim that the text exists to confirm you.

**Third, return to the situation.** What would change in the next conversation, design decision, or research paragraph if your first interpretation were provisional? The text has become useful when it alters the quality of attention, not when it supplies a new identity.

This practice can be done without adopting a religious identity and without treating the *Zhuangzi* as secular self-help. It is a way of allowing a source to remain historically particular while still asking what work its images can do now.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the fasting of the heart in Zhuangzi?

It is the practice described in chapter 4 as *xinzhai*, usually translated “fasting of the mind” or “fasting of the heart-mind.” In context, it is preparation for entering a dangerous human situation without being governed by the self's fixed opinions. The text associates it with listening beyond ordinary sensory and conceptual reception and with an empty, receptive heart-mind.

### Does fasting the mind mean stopping thoughts?

Not necessarily. That is a modern simplification. The passage is better read as loosening the grip of premature interpretation than as suppressing all mental activity. Perception, judgment, memory, and discernment remain necessary. The practice changes their authority and sequence.

### What is “sitting and forgetting”?

*Sitting and forgetting* (*zuowang*) appears in chapter 6 of the *Zhuangzi*. It describes a radical loosening of bodily and conceptual identification in a dialogue between Yan Hui and Confucius. It is related to, but not identical with, the fasting of the heart-mind. The text does not provide a modern standardized meditation program under that name.

### Is Zhuangzi's practice the same as mindfulness?

There are useful comparisons, especially around attention and non-attachment, but the traditions are historically different. “Mindfulness” names multiple modern and Buddhist contexts; *xinzhai* belongs to the literary and philosophical world of the *Zhuangzi*. Comparison should illuminate differences as well as similarities.

### Can this practice treat anxiety or other health conditions?

This essay does not make a medical claim. A reflective pause or careful reading may be personally useful, but it is not a substitute for medical care, therapy, medication, or crisis support. Stop any exercise that increases distress or disconnection and seek appropriate help.

## The discipline of not filling the room

The strongest claim in the fasting passage is also the least spectacular. A human being can be intelligent, informed, ethical in intention, and still fail to meet the world because the self has filled the room before anyone else arrives.

The room fills with a plan for what the conversation must produce. It fills with the identity of the expert who cannot be corrected. It fills with an inherited image of what “Taoist practice” must look like. It fills with a quote stripped from its chapter, a translation treated as final, a physiological promise smuggled into a metaphor. It fills with the desire to have already arrived.

Fasting makes a small vacancy in that room. The vacancy does not tell us what is true. It creates the conditions under which something other than our first preference can become visible.

This is why the practice belongs in an archive as much as in a meditation room. Research requires a willingness to be contradicted by the primary text. Interpretation requires enough emptiness to let a word retain its ambiguity. Comparison requires the discipline not to make every tradition a mirror of our own. Even the act of preserving esoteric material depends on this interval: the archive is not valuable because it agrees with the seeker, but because it allows the seeker to encounter forms that resist easy agreement.

Zhuangzi's heart-mind is not a container waiting to be filled with the correct secret. It is a chamber whose usefulness depends on not being occupied entirely by the interpreter. The point is not to abolish the self. It is to make the self less monopolistic.

That is a serious practice for an overloaded age. It asks less for a new belief than for a more honest order of operations: receive, notice the first grip, allow the situation to remain larger than the interpretation, and act in a way that can still be revised. The ancient phrase does not promise that the world will become quiet. It asks whether we can stop making our first noise the world's final form.
