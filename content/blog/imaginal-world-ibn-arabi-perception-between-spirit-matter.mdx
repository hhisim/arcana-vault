# The Imaginal World: Ibn Arabi and the Discipline of Perception Between Spirit and Matter

### Imagination is not the opposite of reality. Undisciplined, it is escape; disciplined, it is an organ of sight.

The [Universal Transmissions companion essay](https://www.universal-transmissions.com/journal/imaginal-world-ibn-arabi-perception-between-spirit-matter) takes this same material into the studio: how makers, artists, and builders of systems can train imaginal perception as a craft discipline. This Vault of Arcana edition stays with the doctrine and its scholarship — what Ibn Arabi actually claimed about the world between spirit and matter, how Henry Corbin named it for the West, how William Chittick mapped it, and what practice it demands.

Modern English did something violent to the word "imagination." It made it the opposite of the real. A child has an imagination, meaning she invents what is not there. A serious person deals with facts. Somewhere between Romantic poetry and the psychology of fantasy, imagination was demoted from a faculty of knowledge to a faculty of invention — valuable for art, suspect everywhere else, and never, under any circumstances, a way of finding out what is true.

That demotion would have astonished Muhyi al-Din Ibn Arabi (1165–1240), the Andalusian Sufi known to his tradition as al-Shaykh al-Akbar, the Greatest Master. For Ibn Arabi, imagination — *khayal* — is not only a human faculty but a cosmic order. There is, he argues, a real world between the world of pure spirit and the world ofdense matter, a world that is neither and both: the *Alam al-Mithal*, the World of Images, what the French scholar Henry Corbin would call the *mundus imaginalis*. It is, in the careful formulation preserved by scholarship, "a world that is ontologically as real as the world of the senses and that of the intellect." And the human being perceives it with imagination — not imagination as daydream, but imagination as a disciplined organ of perception with its own objects, its own rigor, and its own way of being right or wrong.

That is the thesis of this essay. The imaginal is not the imaginary. The World of Images is not a metaphor for inner life but a middle region of reality, more real than the physical senses report and less real than pure intellect beholds. The soul itself lives there. Dreams, visions, angelic apparitions, the posthumous states, bodily resurrection — phenomena that philosophers before and since have explained away — require it. And entering it knowingly demands training: courtesy, attention, the refusal of both fantasy and literalism. Between spirit and matter stands an isthmus, and the crossing is a discipline.

Readers coming from this site's earlier Sufi essays should note the difference. [The Path of Divine Love](/blog/sufism-the-path-of-divine-love) follows Rumi, longing, and annihilation in the divine. [Sufism and Hermeticism](/blog/sufism-hermeticism-as-above-so-below) works the correspondence of mirror and cosmos. [The Conference of the Birds](/blog/conference-of-the-birds-simurgh-map-of-self-recognition) walks Attar's valleys toward self-recognition. This essay is about none of those things directly. It is about epistemology and ontology: what imagination *is*, what world it perceives, and how one learns to see with it. The vocabulary overlaps; the question is new.

## The heart with two eyes

Ibn Arabi's starting point is anthropological before it is cosmological. In Koranic terms, as the Stanford Encyclopedia's Ibn Arabi entry (authored by William Chittick, the field's leading Anglophone scholar) sets out, the locus of awareness is the heart — *qalb*, a word carrying the verbal sense of fluctuation and turning. And "the heart has two eyes, reason and imagination, and the dominance of either distorts perception and awareness."

Hold that sentence. It says that rationality alone, unchecked, is a distortion — not merely incomplete but actively warping. The rational path of philosophers and theologians, the entry explains, needs complementing by the mystical intuition of the Sufis: the "unveiling" (*kashf*) that allows for "imaginal — not 'imaginary' — vision." The heart in itself is unitary awareness, but it must become attuned to its own fluctuation: "seeing at one beat God's incomparability with the eye of reason, at the next beat his similarity with the eye of imagination."

This double vision is anchored in the two primary names of revelation itself: *al-qur'an*, "that which brings together," and *al-furqan*, "that which differentiates." The first alludes to the unifying oneness of Existence, perceived by imagination; the second to the differentiating manyness of knowledge and discernment, perceived by reason. The Real, as Ibn Arabi constantly repeats, is the One and the Many — One in Essence, many in names. "With the eye of imagination, the heart sees Existence present and immanent in all things, and with the eye of reason it discerns its transcendence and the diversity of the divine faces."

The consequence is exacting and worth stating plainly: anyone who sees only transcendence has half-blinded themselves with reason, and anyone who sees only immanence has half-blinded themselves with imagination. Monotheism without image becomes remote abstraction; immanence without discernment becomes confusion. The heart's discipline is to keep both eyes open and to know which eye is seeing at any moment. That is already a practice, and everything that follows is its elaboration.

## Khayal: closer to "image" than to "imagination"

Much of the modern misunderstanding dissolves once the Arabic is heard. When Ibn Arabi speaks of imagination as one of the heart's two eyes, he uses the established philosophical language of the soul's faculties — but, as Chittick's entry stresses, he is more concerned with imagination's *ontological status*, about which the early philosophers had little to say. "Here his use of *khayal* accords with its everyday meaning, which is closer to image than imagination. The word was employed to designate not only the mental faculty, but also mirror images, shadows, scarecrows, and everything that appears in dreams and visions; in this sense it is synonymous with the term *mithal*, which was often preferred by later authors when speaking of its ontological status."

A mirror image. A shadow. A scarecrow. A dream. These are Ibn Arabi's paradigm cases of the imaginal, and meditating on them teaches more than any definition. He stresses, the entry reports, "that an image brings together two sides and unites them as one; it is both the same as and different from the two. A mirror image is both the mirror and the object that it reflects; or, it is neither the mirror nor the object. A dream is both the soul and what is seen; or, it is neither the soul nor what is seen. By nature images are/are not."

This is the logic of the in-between, and it breaks the logic of reason without violating it. In the eye of reason, a notion is either true or false. "Imagination perceives notions as images and recognizes that they are simultaneously true and false, or neither true nor false." Every tree, stone, face, and verse partakes of this structure. Ibn Arabi's most compressed formula for it: each thing is *He/not He* (*huwa/la huwa*) — Real and unreal, Existence and nonexistence, Face and veil at once. Everything without exception is God's face, revealing certain attributes, and God's veil, concealing others. To perceive a thing imaginally is to hold both truths without letting either cancel the other: the thing is the Real made manifest, and the thing is not the Real but its passing form.

Treat it as a library for verification, not as authority for any claim made here.

## Three worlds of imagination

From this principle Ibn Arabi unfolds not one but three "worlds of imagination," and the distinctions matter because they locate the World of Images precisely.

In the broadest sense, imagination designates everything other than God — the entire cosmos insofar as it is contingent and fleeting. This is "Nondelimited Imagination" (*al-khayal al-mutlaq*): every articulated word of the All-Merciful Breath disclosing Existence in limited form, each thing simultaneously face and veil. In this sense the whole created order is imaginal — is/is-not — and the cosmos itself is, in one of Ibn Arabi's most startling formulations, manifest within imagination: "It is it, and it is not it."

In the narrower and most famous sense, imagination denotes what Corbin calls the *mundus imaginalis*. Islam, like most traditions, conceives the cosmos as a hierarchy of worlds — the Koran contrasts the Unseen (*ghayb*) with the Visible (*shahada*), typically elaborated as the world of spirits and the world of bodies, or in philosophical language the intelligible and the sensible realms. But the Koran also speaks of "heaven, earth, and everything in between" — and one of Ibn Arabi's signal contributions was to draw out the full implications of that *in-between*: a domain "which in one respect is unseen, spiritual, and intelligible, and in another respect visible, corporeal, and sensible."

This is the Alam al-Mithal proper. In it, "spiritual beings are corporealized, as when Gabriel appeared in human form to the Virgin Mary; and corporeal beings are spiritualized, as when bodily pleasure or pain is experienced in the posthumous realms." It is "a real, external realm in the Cosmic Book, more real than the visible, sensible, physical realm, but less real than the invisible, intelligible, spiritual realm." Only its actual existence, Ibn Arabi insists, "can account for angelic and demonic apparitions, bodily resurrection, visionary experience, and other nonphysical yet sensory phenomena that the earlier philosophers typically explained away."

The third world of imagination belongs to the microcosmic human book: it is identical with the soul or self (*nafs*), "the meeting place of spirit (*ruh*) and body (*jism*)." Human experience is always imaginal — simultaneously spiritual and bodily. "Human becoming wavers between spirit and body, light and darkness, wakefulness and sleep, knowledge and ignorance, virtue and vice. Only because the soul dwells in an in-between realm can it choose to strive for transformation and realization. Only as an imaginal reality can it travel 'up' toward the luminosity of the spirit or 'down' toward the darkness of matter."

Three nested claims, then: the cosmos is imaginal, there is an imaginal world between spirit and matter, and the human soul is itself an imaginal reality stationed exactly at the crossing. Anthropology, cosmology, and soteriology turn out to be one structure viewed from three sides. And that structure is what rescues Islamic philosophy, in Chittick's judgment, from "the trap of a mind/body dichotomy or a dualistic worldview" — the in-between is not a problem to be solved but the place where everything actually happens.

## Barzakh: the isthmus that is neither shore

The Koranic word for this in-between is *barzakh* — isthmus, barrier, the partition between two seas that keeps each from overwhelming the other. Ibn Arabi devotes some of his most concentrated writing to it in the eschatological chapters (61–65) of his magnum opus, *al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya* (The Meccan Illuminations), and above all in chapter 63, available in James Morris's translation as "Divine Imagination and the Intermediate World." Its opening definition should be memorized: "Know that the word 'barzakh' is an expression for what separates two things without ever becoming either of them, such as the line separating a shadow from the sunlight."

The line separating shadow from sunlight. Not the shadow, not the sunlight — the line. The barzakh is not a mixture of its two sides but a third reality with its own laws, "neither essential-realities, nor (mere) effects," in the chapter's phrase. It has "wide-open manifestations in being, without restriction." And its breadth is total: "the breadth of this 'Horn' (of the Barzakh Imagination) is as broad as possible, since none of the generated things are broader than It."

Chapter 63 then makes the staggering ontological claim that the Stanford entry quotes from the *Futuhat* (1:306): "Imagination is the widest known thing" because "it exercises its properties through its reality over every thing and non-thing. It gives form to absolute nonexistence, the impossible, the Necessary, and possibility; it makes existence nonexistent and nonexistence existent." Morris's rendering agrees: "It gives form (alike) to absolute nothingness and to what is (ontologically) impossible, necessary and contingent, turning what is existent into nothingness and what is non-existent into being."

Read slowly, this says that imagination — divine and human, for the two interpenetrate — is wider than being itself. Reason sorts the possible from the impossible; imagination gives form to both, and to the Necessary besides. That is why no purely rational metaphysics can close the account of reality: the barzakh contains dimensions that "do not properly belong to the world of intellection," as Chittick puts it — "all the intermediary realms, not to speak of the sensible realm itself, are essentially imaginal, not intelligible." A philosophy that admits only the intelligible is not more rigorous than Ibn Arabi's. It is less complete, having discarded the majority of dimensions.

There is a devotional corollary tucked inside chapter 63 that turns the whole doctrine into practice. The Prophet's saying "Worship God as though you see Him" is, Ibn Arabi explains, an instruction in imaginal perception: imagine the Real in the direction of prayer, be fully mindful and humble, "observe the appropriate courtesy (*adab*) with Him." The *as though* — the chapter calls it the essential reality of the imaginal authority — is not pretense. It is the precise mode in which the in-between is entered: neither claiming literal sight nor settling for abstraction, but holding the image with courtesy until it holds you. *Adab*, exact conduct toward the real, is the method of the imaginal. Without it there is no perception, only projection.

## Corbin's naming: the imaginal against the imaginary

If Ibn Arabi built the doctrine, Henry Corbin gave the West the word that prevents its constant mistranslation. In his landmark Eranos lecture "Mundus Imaginalis, or the Imaginary and the Imaginal," Corbin drew the line that this essay has been walking throughout: the imaginal is a real order of being; the imaginary is the unreal product of fantasy. Between the world of sense and the world of intellect, he argued from the Persian Sufi and theosophical sources, "there is a world that is both intermediary and intermediate, described by our authors as the *'alam al-mithal*, the world of the image, the *mundus imaginalis*: a world that is ontologically as real as the world of the senses and that of the intellect."

Corbin's technical vocabulary repays attention. The Alam al-Mithal is also, in the school of the Illuminationist Suhrawardi, "the world of 'Images in suspense'" — forms that subsist without needing a material substrate, the way a mirror image subsists without being *in* the silver. And "imaginative perception and imaginative consciousness have their function and their noetic (cognitive) value within their own world" — *noetic*, meaning genuinely knowledge-yielding, not decorative. Corbin's life's work, from *Avicenna and the Visionary Recital* (1960) to *Creative Imagination in the Sufism of Ibn Arabi* (1969, in Ralph Manheim's translation; reissued as *Alone with the Alone*), was the defense of this middle cognition against a modernity that recognizes only sensation below and abstraction above.

The stakes of the distinction are practical, not merely scholarly. Confuse the imaginal with the imaginary and two opposite errors follow. The literalist denies every vision, apparition, and dream-annunciation as hallucination, flattening the cosmos into matter plus concepts. The fantasist indulges every private image as revelation, flattening the cosmos into the ego's theater. The imaginal path refuses both: its perceptions are real and therefore *answerable* — to tradition, to a guide, to the Law that scales them, to their fruits in character. An undisciplined image is not a window but a mirror facing inward. That is what discipline is for.

## Chittick's map: a metaphysics of imagination

The Anglophone reader's bridge into all of this is William C. Chittick's *The Sufi Path of Knowledge: Ibn al-'Arabi's Metaphysics of Imagination* (State University of New York Press, 1989) — the book that, as its publisher's record notes of its subject, takes "a major step toward exposing the breadth and depth of Ibn al-'Arabi's vision," presenting a thinker "famous in the West as the greatest mystical thinker of Islamic civilization." Chittick's method is itself imaginal in spirit: rather than paraphrasing the Master into modern jargon, he lets Ibn Arabi speak, organizing more than six hundred passages from the *Futuhat* — many in English for the first time, amounting to roughly twice the bulk of the *Fusus* — into the architecture of a complete metaphysics: theology, ontology, epistemology, hermeneutics, soteriology, consummation.

Two chapters sit at this essay's center. Chapter 7, "Cosmic Imagination" — He/Not He, Imagination, Dreams, the Manifestation of the Impossible — lays out the doctrine that the cosmos itself is God's imagining, with dreams as the nightly evidence every human being is given: in sleep you inhabit a world that is neither your mind alone nor the outer senses, and you call it real while you are there. Chapter 8, "The Supreme Barzakh" — the Cloud, the Breath of the All-Merciful — treats the highest isthmus: the All-Merciful Breath (*nafas al-rahman*) exhaling the worlds the way breath carries words, each existent a word articulated in mercy, neither identical with the Breath nor other than it.

The primary text behind both chapters is worth meeting directly. Ibn Arabi's *Fusus al-Hikam* (The Bezels of Wisdom), his most concentrated work — twenty-seven chapters, one for each prophetic wisdom — survives in open English translation on the Internet Archive, where its record ("Topics sufism, Collection opensource") makes the Master's own voice freely checkable. Its opening seal already states the epistemology this essay has unfolded: "the intellect cannot perceive this fact by means of logical investigation for this sort of perception only exists through divine unveiling." Reason investigates; unveiling perceives. The *Fusus* is the unveiling set down in words — which is why it must be read, as Chittick insists, with both eyes open.

## Why the in-between cannot be discarded

It is worth asking, skeptically, what work the Imaginal World actually does — what breaks if it is removed. Ibn Arabi's answer is a catalogue, and Chittick's entry preserves it: "angelic and demonic apparitions, bodily resurrection, visionary experience, and other nonphysical yet sensory phenomena." Consider each.

Dreams: every night the soul leaves the senses and inhabits forms — places, persons, events — that act on it with full sensory force and sometimes with prophetic truth. If only matter and mind exist, dreams must be either neural noise or abstract messages. The imaginal account says they are perceptions of a real middle world, truer or falser according to the dreamer's state — which is why traditions take some dreams as guidance and dismiss others, instead of either worshipping or ignoring all of them.

Death: the posthumous states described by every revelation involve sensory experience — pleasure, pain, encounter, place — without a physical body. Either the descriptions are poetry for the credulous, or there is a realm where "corporeal beings are spiritualized," where experience continues in forms subtler than flesh but denser than pure thought. The barzakh *is* that realm in Islamic eschatology — the isthmus between this world and resurrection that Morris's chapter 63 expounds — and the soul's nightly dreaming is its rehearsal.

Apparition: Gabriel appearing in human form to Mary is the Koranic paradigm — a spiritual being corporealized, perceivable by the senses yet not reducible to them. Every tradition carries such reports. The philosophers, Ibn Arabi charges, "typically explained away" such phenomena because their two-storey cosmos had no room for them. The three-storey cosmos does: the in-between is exactly where spirit takes body and body takes spirit.

Resurrection: the bodily resurrection affirmed by the Abrahamic faiths is neither the resuscitation of the corpse nor a metaphor for memory. It is the imaginal body — the "body of resurrection" of Corbin's great study — the form the soul's imaginal reality assumes when its state becomes its place. Without the middle world, resurrection is incoherent; with it, it is the strict consequence of the soul being what it is.

None of this compels belief. It compels a choice: either a cosmos too thin to house the experiences human beings actually report across every century and continent, or a cosmos with a middle term that houses them and disciplines their reading. Ibn Arabi's wager is that thinness is the fantasy, and fullness the rigor.

## The discipline: how perception is trained

Doctrine without practice is decoration, and the imaginal doctrine carries its practice inside it. Five disciplines, one per finger of the grasping hand.

**Open both eyes.** Begin where the heart begins: notice, in any act of understanding, which eye is open. Are you discerning differences — transcendent, differentiating, furqanic? Or are you beholding presence — unifying, gathering, koranic? The dominance of either distorts. The daily exercise is alternation: read a verse, a face, a landscape twice, once for what sets it apart and once for what shines through it, and refuse to let either reading abolish the other.

**Keep the dream-book.** Dreams are the one imaginal experience granted to everyone without initiation, and every tradition of this path treats them as the textbook. Record them. Learn, over months, which of your dreams carried weight and which carried only the day's residue — the tradition is explicit that both kinds exist. The point is not interpretation as cleverness but calibration as honesty: discovering that you already perceive imaginally every night, badly, and can learn to do it better. Chittick's chapter on dreams in "Cosmic Imagination" is the scholarly companion to this work.

**Practice the *as though*.** "Worship God as though you see Him." Extend the instruction beyond formal prayer: approach every act of attention — reading, listening, making, meeting — as though the Real were present in it, with the courtesy (*adab*) owed to a presence. This is not pretending. It is the imaginal mode itself: holding the image neither as literal fact nor as empty fiction, but as the isthmus where encounter happens. The courtesy is the method. Discourtesy — rushing, irony, grasping, contempt — collapses the isthmus from the human side, and then nothing appears, which proves nothing except the discourtesy.

**Submit images to the scale.** The tradition surrounds imaginal perception with checks: the revealed Law, the guide, the community, the fruits. An authentic perception increases mercy, humility, and exactness; a fantasy increases self-importance, license, or fear. Weigh every strong image by its harvest. This is why the path pairs unveiling (*kashf*) with the Scale of the Law — Chittick devotes a full chapter to the pairing — and why solitary traffic with visions, unguided and unweighed, is counted among the path's classic dangers rather than its attainments.

**Travel up, not down.** The soul stationed between spirit and matter can move either way: toward the luminosity of spirit or toward the darkness of matter. Both movements use images. The difference is orientation — whether the image renders the perceiver more transparent to the Real or more opaque, more merciful or more cruel. Ask of any sustained imaginative practice, artistic or contemplative, which direction it is carrying you. The answer is usually legible within weeks, in conduct before it is legible in visions.

The closing question, shared with the companion essay, is the whole of the doctrine in one line: can you perceive the image without either worshipping it or explaining it away?

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the Imaginal World real or symbolic?

In Ibn Arabi's doctrine it is real — "ontologically as real as the world of the senses and that of the intellect," in Corbin's formulation — and for that very reason symbolic: its forms carry meanings that exceed their appearances. "Real or symbolic" is a false choice produced by the two-storey cosmos. The three-storey cosmos answers: real, and therefore inexhaustibly meaningful.

### What is the difference between khayal and fantasy?

*Khayal* in Ibn Arabi's technical sense is a faculty of perception with real objects (the forms of the middle world) and disciplines of verification (the Law, the guide, the fruits). Fantasy is the same faculty undisciplined — generating images answerable to nothing but desire. The difference is not in vividness but in accountability.

### What does barzakh mean?

Literally an isthmus or barrier — "what separates two things without ever becoming either of them, such as the line separating a shadow from the sunlight." Technically it names every intermediary reality, supremely the Imaginal World between spirit and matter, and eschatologically the state between death and resurrection.

### Who was Henry Corbin?

A French philosopher and scholar of Islamic thought (1903–1978) whose translations and studies — above all *Creative Imagination in the Sufism of Ibn Arabi* (1969) — introduced the Alam al-Mithal to Western philosophy under the name *mundus imaginalis*, insisting on the imaginal/imaginary distinction this essay follows.

### Who was William Chittick?

The leading Anglophone scholar of Ibn Arabi, author of *The Sufi Path of Knowledge: Ibn al-'Arabi's Metaphysics of Imagination* (1989) and of the Stanford Encyclopedia's Ibn Arabi entry, whose translations of the *Futuhat* made the Greatest Master's account of imagination available in English at scale.
